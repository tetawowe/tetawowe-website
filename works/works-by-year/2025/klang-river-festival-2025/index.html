Klang River Festival (KRF) is an annual open festival, presented by KongsiKL, aiming to bring local communities and stakeholders together to share knowledge and facilitate discourse on heritage, culture and identity of rivers. Recognising the transient and ever-changing nature of the locales along the Klang River and its tributaries, the festival highlights the river's importance as a source for transportation, fresh water, food, agriculture, and religious rituals through three pillars - People, Habitat, and Culture. Serving as an open platform, KRF unites individuals, NGOs, government agencies, and private sectors to pursue sustainable socio-ecological benefits, creating equilibrium between the needs, desires, and priorities of various stakeholders in an urban intervention. This ensures that no single group dominates the decision-making process, fostering fair, inclusive, and sustainable urban development. More than just a festival, Klang River Festival calls on people to reminisce, revisit and rekindle their sense of belonging and nostalgia with the Klang River all year round, through activations, activities, events, workshops and more. By reclaiming spatial agency along the riverside, the locals are empowered as spatial operators, fostering a sense of belonging and responsibility for the river. The long term goal of Klang River Festival is to create a Klang River network, one that unites the communities centered around the riverside. With a 10-year road map set out, KRF hopes to bring the communities closer together, with the river as its guiding path, creating a new Klang River network.

When we first approached the riverside, the tranquil and serene riverscape gave a sense of calm amidst the bustling urban environment. Despite being next to two major roads, Old Klang Road and New Pantai Expressway, the riverside felt unusually calm. The locals have long understood the nature of the river and its power, when the area was constantly under threat of floods. Through interventions and infrastructure upgrades, floodings have become few and far between. As we walk along the riverside, one constant thought comes to mind: “What can we do here to make it more pleasing for everyone?” The idea of a linear river park springs to mind. Walking along the riverside, we envisioned the river as a shared space for all. Its edges and structures adapt to changing programs, allowing spatial operators to shape and activate the river’s margins. Inspired by Bernard Tschumi’s follies at Parc de la Villette, the River Acrobats function as organisational points along the river—repetitive yet distinct, guiding visitors and anchoring a sense of place without fixed programs. Reimagined as guardians of the riverside, each acrobat carries memories of past river-based activities and trades, from rubber smokehouses and fishing to jetties, dobi, and childhood play. Constructed from materials collected on site, they reconstruct fragments of the river’s history. Through their ability to morph and hold space, the River Acrobats transform the riverside into a living archive of memory and meaning.
[Rubber Smokehouse] Rubber used to be the main export for Malaya, and many rubber plantations and smokehouses were located along the riverside at the outskirts of the city, utilising the river as the transport route. One such rubber smokehouse was located along Old Klang Road, before being demolished in 2018. The smokehouse was a significant part of the rubber industry. Workers hung the rubber sheets high on a bamboo pole and fumigated it with fire inside the smokehouse. In between, the rubber sheets are turned to ensure it is evenly smoked and golden, and it is ready to be sold to processors to make other rubber products. The acrobat carries the memories of the past, shouldering the rubber sheets within itself, gently swaying in the wind.
[The Jetty] As the highway of the past, rivers were the main trading route for inland towns, creating a river network that was important for the livelihoods of the towns. Jetties were important trading hubs, with markets and shops set up around the jetty. One famous example is Central Market, which was located near Masjid Jamek before moving to its current location. Many of these jetties are gone in urban townships, and with it our connection to the riverside. We want to reestablish the connection with our rivers, extending our arms into the river, connecting once again with our nature. The acrobat sits atop a concrete ledge, extending towards the river. Various items such as tools and baskets remind us of the trades of days past.
[Angler’s Deck] Fishing is a daily hobby of the locals, with many of the local residents fishing under the highway. We joined the anglers on their fishing, seeing the river and nature as a resource for our consumption. Yet we wondered as we looked down into the river at the fishes, what do the fishes see and think? Turning our point of view, we reimagined our place by the river to one underneath our river, with the fishes swimming above us. It is the fishes that look at us, thinking “why don’t you join me in the water?”
[Fortress of Play] One day after a site walk, we saw the children from PPR Seri Anggerik and Taman Hock Ann playing by the busy road. The lack of open space meant that there was no safe space for the children to play and spend time in. The riverside provides a good opportunity for play spaces amongst nature. Like a blanket fort, the acrobat transforms into a fortress of play, sheltering the children, and creating a safe space for them to be children. The children are also able to express themselves with the blank canvases, not only through play, but also through communal creation.
[Dobi] Another long forgotten trade by the river are the dhobi wallahs (washers). The dhobis soaked and washed along the river, slapped the laundry against washstones, and hung them on lines to dry. Once a lucrative trade, many are lost to time, with only a handful still operating in wooden structures by the river. The rivers too have become too polluted to be used for washing. Evoking the colourful and varied washing that happened by the rivers, the acrobat extends itself to different corners along the river, even across the bank. It collects the clothes of the neighbours, displaying the washing proudly. It shows within itself a lost trade, frozen in time.
